# Bee Home — revival brief

Working notes for rebuilding the Bee Home 3D visualiser / builder that used to live at
`beehome.design`. Everything here is either extracted from the files in this repo or
sourced from third-party coverage of the original launch (May 2020). Links at the bottom.

---

## 1. What the original was

SPACE10 (IKEA's now-closed research and design lab) released Bee Home on World Bee Day,
20 May 2020, as a free, CC BY 4.0 open-source design for solitary-bee hotels. The point
was less the bee hotel and more the distribution model: a parametric product you
configure in the browser, download as cutting files, and have fabricated at a local
makerspace or CNC shop.

Roles, per the repo README and `ReadMe.pdf`:

| Role | Who |
| --- | --- |
| Project owner | SPACE10 |
| Physical design | Tanita Klein |
| Grasshopper definition | ChipChop |
| Online parametric engine | ShapeDiver |
| Digital interface / identity | Bakken & Bæck |
| Photography | Irina Boersma |

### The original stack

Bakken & Bæck's write-up describes the chain as **Grasshopper → ShapeDiver → three.js**:

- The Grasshopper definition (`BEEHOME.gh`, in this repo) is the source of truth for
  geometry and for generating nested 2D cutting files.
- ShapeDiver hosted that definition as a cloud parametric service, so the website could
  request a configuration without running Rhino.
- three.js rendered the result in the browser, with **AR viewing** as an explicit goal —
  "view the model from all angles, both in 3D and augmented reality".

That's the piece worth rethinking on a revival: ShapeDiver is a paid hosted dependency,
and the whole configuration space here is small and enumerable (see §3). A modern rebuild
can ship the storey library as static glTF and do the assembly maths client-side, keeping
Grasshopper only for regenerating the source geometry and the CNC nesting.

## 2. What's already in this repo (the useful part)

`BEEHOME GEOMETRIES.3dm` contains two layers that are literally the original web assets.
A `REFERENCE` text object in the file names them:

> `WEBSITE 3D geometries (.obj export)`

So the original viewer was fed OBJ exports of these layers. Contents:

**`WEBSITE - Model`** — 40 solids, laid out as two vertical stacks of 16, matching the
16 storey letters A–P:

- **Stack at x ≈ 8600** — 16 uniform bodies, all **120 × 160 mm** footprint,
  heights 30–36 mm. These are the `DEFAULT` storeys.
- **Stack at x ≈ 8000** — 16 bodies with the same heights but varying footprints
  (120, 132, 138, 144 mm wide; 160–192 mm deep). The overhangs read as the `ROOF` /
  `FIXED` variants — `ReadMe.pdf` names exactly those three geometry groups
  (`ROOF`, `FIXED`, `DEFAULT`) when it explains re-referencing geometry in Grasshopper.

**`WEBSITE - Guides`** — the three mounting options, labelled in the file:

| Label | Geometry | Maps to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Base` | 140 × 160 × 30 mm plate (×3) | the plinth under the stack |
| `Legs` | 15 × 30 × 50 mm × 4 | position `01` Standing |
| `Spike` | 30 × 30 × 200 mm | position `02` Grounded |

A dump of every object with its bounding box is in `docs/website-geometry.json`,
regenerated by `tools/extract_website_geometry.py`.

**Caveat:** the 3dm stores these as Breps/Extrusions with no render meshes attached, so
`rhino3dm` alone can read dimensions but cannot tessellate them. Producing glTF needs
either Rhino/`Rhino.Compute`, or a headless mesher. The stack ordering (which body is A,
which is P) is inferred from vertical position and still needs a visual check against the
`REFERENCE` layer's `A . B. C. … P` label row.

Also present: `BEEHOME Components.3dm` (63 objects — the spine/key joinery parts),
`Production Stock.3dm` (stock sheet + work area for nesting), and `PLUGINS.zip`
(the Grasshopper plugins the definition depends on).

## 3. The configuration model — fully specified

This is the best find in the repo. `ReadMe.pdf` documents the complete parameter space as
a **Bee Home ID** string, which is what the website generated and handed to Grasshopper.

```
XX  xxxx   xxxx
│   │      └── storeys, in letters (A–P), bottom-to-top
│   └───────── height in mm
└───────────── position
```

| Position | Code | Height field |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standing (on legs) | `01` | digits, e.g. `200` |
| Grounded (on spike) | `02` | digits, e.g. `1150` |
| Fixed (wall/rooftop mounted) | `03` | *n/a — omitted* |

Examples from the PDF:

- `01196APPM` — standing, 196 mm tall, 4 storeys A-P-P-M
- `03NOPJNO` — fixed, 6 storeys
- `021000AB` — grounded, 1000 mm tall, 2 storeys

### Storey sets

Storeys ship as sets of 1–3 that can also be split apart:
`A+B`, `C`, `D+E+F`, `G+H`, `I+J`, `K+L`, `M`, `N+O+P`.

### Stacking rules

Cavity positions mean not every storey can sit on every other. The PDF gives the rules
twice with small discrepancies between the body text and the diagram caption — the two
versions are transcribed below and **need reconciling against the Grasshopper definition
before they're encoded as a validator.**

Version A (body text, p05):

```
A not on D,E,F,G,H        I not on D,E,F,G,H
B not on D,E,F,G,H        J not on D,E,F,G,H
C not on D,E,F,G,H        K not on A,D,E,F,G,H,I,N,O
D not on A,G,H,I,J,N,O    L not on A,D,E,F,G,H,I,N,O
E not on A,G,H,I,J,N,O    M not on A,E,F,G,H,I,J,N,O
F not on A,G,H,I,J,N,O    N not on D,E,F,G,H
G not on D,E              O not on D,E,F,G,H
H not on D,E              P not on D,E,F,G,H
```

Version B (diagram caption, p05) — differs on D, E, K, L, M and adds "F, G and H fit on
any":

```
D not on G
E not on A,G,I,J,M
F, G and H fit on any
K not on A,D,E,F,G,H,I,N,O,P
L not on A,D,E,F,G,H,I,N,O,P
M not on O,N
```

Version A is the more conservative superset; start there, verify against `BEEHOME.gh`.

### Why this matters for the rebuild

The whole space is: 3 positions × a height integer × a variable-length word over a
16-letter alphabet with adjacency constraints. That is small, local, and needs no cloud
parametric service. A revived builder can:

1. Load 16 (or 32, with variants) storey meshes once.
2. Stack them by translating along Z using the per-storey heights already tabulated.
3. Validate adjacency client-side from the rules above.
4. Emit the same Bee Home ID string — which keeps the existing Grasshopper file working
   as the fabrication back end, unchanged. **The ID is the compatibility contract with
   the original project; keep it byte-identical.**

## 4. Visual direction

### The physical object

Hardwood throughout — the original deliberately left species open so people used local
timber, typically CNC-cut sheet at 6 mm (the layer names in the 3dm are all
`T6MM`, with one `T5MM` pocket set). Assembly is a **spine-and-key** system inspired by
Japanese wood joinery: storeys thread onto a vertical spine and lock together, so the
whole thing can be dismantled for cleaning without glue or fasteners. The look is
unfinished pale timber, visible end grain, stacked horizontal bands with a rhythm of
drilled cavities of varying diameters, capped by an overhanging roof storey.

The one photo in this repo — `SPACE10-Bee_Home_Makery_Process-Web-Photo_by_Irina_Boersma_04.jpg`,
from the Makery process shoot — is the best in-repo reference for material and finish.

### The digital identity

From Bakken & Bæck's own case description:

- A **logo mark and custom typeface** drawn from "the shape of bees and the rounded form
  of their nests and tunnels" — i.e. soft, tunnel-like rounded terminals, letterforms
  built from circular apertures. That's the single most distinctive element to re-create,
  and the one thing not preserved anywhere in this repo.
- A "playful visual identity … a great balance between fun, fresh design and a DIY feel."
- Research into **how bees interpret colour** fed the palette, plus a drawing exercise
  with children that fed the identity. Bee colour vision is shifted toward UV/blue and
  is red-blind, which is a defensible reason for a palette leaning yellow-green, blue and
  violet rather than the obvious honey-and-black.

### The interface

Reconstructed from multiple write-ups, consistent across sources:

1. Choose **where it goes** — rooftop, backyard/garden, or balcony. This framed the
   configurator and mapped onto the three mounting positions.
2. Choose **height**, **number of storeys**, and **visual expression** (which storey
   letters, i.e. which cavity patterns).
3. Preview in **3D from all angles**, and in **AR** placed in your actual garden.
4. **Download the files free and instantly**, plus printable assembly instructions, and
   find a local fab lab to cut them.

Repeated framing in the coverage: "fun, intuitive, and easy enough to do in a matter of
minutes." That's the bar — a toy, not a CAD tool.

## 5. Gaps and next steps

Open questions:

- **No recovered screenshots or the original typeface.** See the note in §6 — archive
  access was blocked from this session.
- Storey letter ↔ geometry mapping needs a visual confirmation pass in Rhino.
- The two stacking-rule variants need reconciling against `BEEHOME.gh`.
- Height semantics: whether the `height in mm` field is total height including legs/spike
  or the elevation of the stack above ground isn't stated. The `01196APPM` example
  (196 mm, 4 storeys × ~33 mm ≈ 132 mm of storeys) suggests it is *total* height, with
  legs making up the difference — worth confirming.

Suggested build order:

1. Mesh the `WEBSITE - Model` layer to glTF (needs Rhino or Rhino.Compute once); commit
   the 16–32 storey meshes as static assets.
2. Port the Bee Home ID grammar and stacking rules to a small, tested pure module.
3. three.js viewer: stack, orbit, ground plane, three mounting modes.
4. `<model-viewer>` for AR on top of the same glTF — cheap, and restores parity with the
   original.
5. Only then: export path. Either keep Grasshopper as an offline step (user takes the ID
   to the .gh file, which already works) or reimplement 2D nesting.
